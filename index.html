AS3 — Voice Applications

There are many voice-based technologies on the market, such as Amazon Echo (Alexa), Siri, and Google Home. These tools are super convenient for the average everyday user, but they can also help promote accessibility for some disabled individuals, such as people who are visually impaired or have motor disabilities that make it difficult to type or interact with a smart system in the same way someone traditionally would on a laptop or cell phone. However, these tools may be challenging or inaccessible for certain disability demographics. One example is people who are non-speaking or unable to speak, as well as people with progressive neurological conditions that can cause them to lose their ability to speak over time, such as ALS. Since these individuals may not be able to speak to the device, traditional voice interfaces can be difficult for them to use. Additionally, people who are Deaf or hard of hearing may have difficulty interacting with these interfaces because they may be unable to hear the response. Some people who are Deaf or hard of hearing may also have speech differences, such as differences in pronunciation, speech patterns, or accents, that can make it harder for these devices to accurately understand them. People who stutter can also have difficulty with voice assistants, which often treat a pause or repeated sound as the end of a command and cut the user off before they finish. In addition to these more apparent challenges, people with less apparent disabilities, such as cognitive disabilities, ADHD, or memory-related conditions, may also face barriers when using voice interfaces, since the lack of a visual record can make it difficult to follow a longer command or recall what was said earlier in the interaction.

There are already some existing solutions that help address these challenges, particularly for people who are hard of hearing or cannot speak for various reasons. One example is the ability to type commands instead of speaking them. Siri and other voice assistant platforms offer accessibility settings that allow users to type their commands, providing another way to interact with the system without having to speak. Additionally, Siri and other voice assistants can display a transcription of what the system believes the user said on the screen. This provides another outlet beyond relying solely on voice and hearing. For example, someone with a cognitive disability, ADHD, or a memory-related difficulty may benefit from seeing their prompt written on the screen so they can confirm what they said or better keep track of the interaction. Finally, some voice assistants use voice recognition training to better understand an individual’s speech. Users may be asked to repeat a series of prompts so the system can become more familiar with their particular voice, pronunciation, speech patterns, and accent. These features can help improve recognition for people whose speech may differ from the speech patterns that voice recognition systems are typically trained on.

One prototype solution that I had in mind is a tool designed to help individuals who have difficulty speaking and may not be properly understood by voice-based technologies. Despite existing options, such as Siri’s ability to learn and better recognize atypical speech, understanding can still be a major barrier for individuals with certain disabilities. Oftentimes, when a voice assistant does not understand a command, the user is simply asked to repeat themselves, which can be frustrating and especially difficult for someone who has trouble speaking. Instead, this tool would use AI-powered voice recognition to transcribe the parts of the user’s command that it is able to understand and provide a few possible options for any unclear information that the user can select from. For example, if a user says, “Hey Siri, can you send me a reminder for the dentist for 3 p.m. on Tuesday?” and the system is unsure about one part of the command, rather than responding with “I’m sorry, I didn’t catch that,” it could display the information it understood and provide selectable options for the unclear portion. The user could then confirm or correct the command without having to speak again. If any other part of the transcription was misheard, the user could also tap that word to open the same selection process for it, so no portion of the command is locked in without the user’s confirmation. This concept could also be further enhanced for people with motor disabilities who may not be able to tap or select an option with their fingers. For example, the system could eventually support eye-tracking or other alternative input methods for users with limited motor control. Additional enhancements could also incorporate AI-based lip reading to help interpret a user’s intended command and provide options that the user can then choose to confirm, creating additional pathways for accessible interaction. Finish My Sentence

The prototype described in the written response. Press the microphone, speak any short command, and the assistant transcribes what it understood while offering selectable options for any unclear part so the command does not have to be repeated.

AssistantVoice command

What to try

  1. Press the microphone and allow access when your browser asks.
  2. Say any short command: a reminder, a call, a message, a timer, lights, directions.
  3. The assistant shows what it heard and flags one unclear part.
  4. Tap the right option, then confirm. You never speak the whole thing again.

How the unclear part is picked

The browser’s recognizer returns a top guess and a couple of alternatives. The first word that differs between them is the unclear part, and the alternatives become the options. If the speech comes through cleanly, the assistant still flags one word (a name, day, number or time) so you can confirm before anything runs.
